Section A

💨 Smoke Interacting with Objects

🏕️

Real-World Analogy

Picture a campfire with a pot hanging over it. The smoke doesn't pass through the pot — it wraps around it, curling to the sides. In Blender, the pot would be an Effector. It tells the smoke simulation: "You can't go through me — go around!"

How It Works in Blender

Your smoke simulation has three main ingredients:

ComponentWhat It DoesReal-World Equivalent
DomainThe invisible box containing everythingThe air around a campfire
Flow (Torus)Produces the smokeThe burning wood
Effector (Cube)Blocks / deflects the smokeThe pot over the fire

Lab Steps (Section A)

  1. Open your file from the previous lab (your torus inside a domain). Add a Cube via Add → Mesh → Cube and position it just above the torus. Scale it with S + Z.
  2. Select the cube, go to Physics, add Fluid, and set Type to Effector. Leave Effector Type as Collision.
  3. Click the Domain, set Resolution Divisions to 60.
  4. Scroll down and click Bake All (Frame Start: 1, End: 100).
  5. Press Space to play — watch the smoke wrap around the cube!
  6. Try hiding the torus (click the eye icon) to see the effector interaction more clearly.
  7. Save and upload to SEQTA as Lab 18A.blend
Section B

🔥 Creating Fire

🕯️

Real-World Analogy

Think of a gas stove. The burner (Flow Object) doesn't just sit there — it continuously pushes gas upward (Inflow), which ignites into fire (Fuel). The flame's height depends on how much fuel is flowing in, and the heat makes the flame rise. Blender simulates this same physics!

Smoke vs Fire — What Changes?

In Section A you made smoke. To switch to fire, you change just one setting on the Flow Object:

Use Flow✅✅

Lab Steps (Section B)

  1. Click on the Torus from Section A. In Physics → Fluid → Settings, change Flow Type to Fire, set Flow Behavior to Inflow, and tick Use Flow.
  2. Keep Fuel at 1 for now (you can experiment later).
  3. Click the Domain. Set the frame End to 100. Click Free All first, then Bake All.
  4. Press Space to play and observe the fire!
  5. Quality boost: Increase Resolution Divisions to 100 and re-bake.
  6. Save and upload to SEQTA as Lab 18B.blend

⚡ Pro Tip: Resolution vs Bake Time

Resolution is like the pixel count of your simulation. At 60, the smoke looks a bit blocky — like a 480p YouTube video. At 200, it's smooth and cinema-quality — like 4K. But just like rendering a 4K video takes longer, higher resolution means much longer bake times. Start low, get your setup right, then crank it up for the final version!

Section C

🎨 Shaders, Lighting & Rendering

📸

Real-World Analogy

Imagine you're filming smoke in a dark room. Without any lights, you see nothing. Add a bright light from the side and suddenly every wisp and curl becomes visible, creating dramatic shadows. That's exactly what we're doing in Blender — using area lights and shaders to make smoke look cinematic, not just grey blobs.

What is a Shader?

A shader is a set of instructions that tells Blender how a material should look when light hits it. For smoke, the shader controls the colour, how thick (dense) the smoke appears, and how light scatters through it. Blender uses a Principled Volume shader for smoke — it's designed specifically for volumetric (cloud-like) materials.

Lab Steps (Section C)

  1. Start a new project. Delete everything. Add a Torus via Add → Mesh → Torus.
  2. Use Quick Smoke: Object → Quick Effects → Quick Smoke
  3. Click the Domain. Set Resolution Division to 200 (or lower if your PC is slow).
  4. Set End frame to 150, then Bake All. This will take a while — be patient!
  5. Hide the Torus. Switch to Rendered View (Z menu) and press play.
  6. Add an Area Light: Add → Light → Area. Move it with G, rotate Y to -90°, and rotate with R+Z so it faces the domain.
  7. Set the light Power to 1000 and Size to 0.49m.
  8. Click the Smoke Domain → Material tab. Change the Volume colour to green and Density to 7.
  9. Position your Camera in front of the domain using G+X/Y/Z and R+X/Y/Z.
  10. Enable Denoise in Render Settings. Change Focal Length to 60mm.
  11. Set World colour to Black for maximum contrast.
  12. Optional: Render an animation via Render → Render Animation
  13. Save and upload to SEQTA as Lab18C.blend

🔮 Cycles vs EEVEE — What's the Difference?

EEVEE is Blender's real-time renderer — fast but less accurate. Think of it as a game engine preview. Cycles traces every ray of light physically, like a real camera. It's much slower but produces photorealistic results. For smoke and fire, Cycles creates dramatically better results because it accurately simulates light scattering through the volume. If your computer has a good GPU, switch to Render → GPU Compute → Cycles for the best quality.

Q1 — What is the role of the Domain in a smoke simulation?

A It produces the smoke particles
B It defines the bounding box where the simulation takes place
C It controls the colour of the smoke
D It renders the final animation
✅ Correct! The Domain is the invisible container where the entire simulation happens. Nothing exists outside it.
❌ Not quite. The Domain is the invisible bounding box that contains the entire simulation — think of it as the air space around a campfire.

Q2 — What does an Effector do?

A Speeds up the baking process
B Generates fire from any mesh
C Blocks or deflects smoke/fire, forcing it to flow around the object
D Changes the background colour of the scene
✅ Spot on! An Effector is like a pot over a campfire — smoke has to go around it.
❌ An Effector deflects smoke, making it wrap around the object. Think of holding your hand over a candle — the smoke splits around your hand.

Q3 — What does increasing the Resolution Division do?

A Increases the detail/quality of the simulation but takes longer to bake
B Makes the smoke move faster
C Changes the smoke from grey to a colour
D Increases the size of the Domain
✅ Exactly! It's like going from 480p to 4K — more detail, more compute time.
❌ Resolution Division controls simulation quality. Higher values = finer detail but longer bake times. Think of it like video quality — 60 is 480p, 200 is 4K.

Q4 — To switch from smoke to fire, which setting do you change on the Flow Object?

A Domain Type from Gas to Liquid
B Effector Type from Collision to Guide
C Render engine from EEVEE to Cycles
D Flow Type from Smoke to Fire
✅ Correct! It's as simple as changing Flow Type to "Fire" on the Flow Object.
❌ You need to change the Flow Type on the Flow Object (e.g., the Torus) from Smoke to Fire. The Domain stays as Gas.

Q5 — A density value of 3 would look most like:

A Thick bushfire smoke you can't see through
B Light fog or mist
C A solid wall of colour
D Invisible — no smoke visible at all
✅ Nice one! Low density (1-3) creates a thin, transparent fog effect.
❌ Density 3 is quite low — it produces a light, see-through fog effect. You'd need 15+ for thick bushfire-style smoke.

Q6 — Why do we enable "Denoise" when rendering smoke?

A It makes the smoke animation play faster
B It increases the simulation resolution
C It removes grainy speckles from the rendered image for a cleaner result
D It automatically adds fire to the smoke
✅ Correct! Denoise removes the grainy noise that Cycles produces, giving you a much cleaner final image.
❌ Denoise is a post-processing step that removes the grainy/speckled artifacts from Cycles renders, producing a smoother, cleaner image.
