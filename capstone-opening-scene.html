Opening Scene

Twelve seconds that open a game or a film: a car swerves off a dusk road, smashes through crates, hits a tree and bursts into flames under a volcano. Four cameras, cut like a movie, rendered to .mp4.

Start the capstone ↓

Rendered in Blender 5.2 by following the steps on this page.

The brief

This is the project the whole Blender unit has been building up to. You will not learn many new buttons: you will put together the car, the volcano, the fire, the physics and the cameras you already know into one short film.

You need Blender 4.5 or newer (built and tested in 5.2), a mouse with a scroll wheel and a middle button, your Lab11_Car_YourName.blend and Lab17_Volcano_YourName.blend, and about twelve lessons over four weeks. Save as Capstone_OpeningScene_YourName.blend.

You’ve got it when you can

  • bring finished models into a new scene with Append and organise them in collections
  • dress a set: ground, road, a scattered forest with Geometry Nodes, a town, lighting for a time of day
  • keyframe a vehicle crash with the right interpolation, and make a tree react
  • let rigid body physics and a fire simulation react to your animation, and bake them
  • plan four shots, give each camera a lens, a move and a reason, and cut between them with markers
  • render a 12-second widescreen .mp4 and explain the choices you made

How the steps work

Each step has a Where line (which part of the Blender window to look at), an animated drawing of the mouse and keys doing the step (pause it, scrub it, or use the arrow buttons to go one move at a time; Larger opens it full screen), and a Do this list of the same moves as a, b, c. Do one move, look at the drawing, do the next. Tick the number when the step is done: your progress is saved in this browser.

The drawings are redrawn Blender, not screenshots: the words, numbers and order match Blender 5.2; colours and spacing are close. A gold outline marks a box you are typing into. Yellow, green and orange boxes mean what they mean in Blender: keyed, animated, changed-but-not-keyed.

Blender 4.2 to 4.5? Everything works the same except two places, both flagged in the steps: Step 51 (File Format › FFmpeg Video instead of Media Type) and Step 52 (Viewport Render Animation).

Word bank: open this if a word in the steps is new
Append
Copy objects, collections or materials out of another .blend file into this one. The originals stay where they were.
Collection
A folder in the Outliner. A whole car can travel as one collection.
Clip End
How far a viewport or camera can see. Anything further away is not drawn.
Aspect ratio
The shape of the frame. 1920 × 816 is 2.35 : 1, the cinema “scope” shape.
Geometry Nodes
A modifier built from nodes that makes or changes shapes, like scattering trees.
Instance
A copy that shares its mesh with the original. Hundreds of instances cost almost nothing.
Linked duplicate (Alt+D)
A copy that shares its mesh: edit one, all change. Scale and position stay separate.
Interpolation
How Blender fills the frames between keys. Bézier eases in and out; Linear keeps a constant speed.
Animated (rigid body)
A Passive rigid body that follows its keyframes and pushes Active bodies out of its way.
Domain
The box a smoke or fire simulation happens inside. Nothing exists outside it.
Flow
The object smoke or fire comes out of. Fuel sets how much fire it makes.
Bake
Calculate a simulation once and save it, so playback and render always match.
Empty
An object with no shape: a point to aim at, or a handle to turn.
Track To
A constraint that keeps an object, usually a camera, pointed at a target.
Focal length
The lens. Small numbers (18–28 mm) are wide; big ones (85–135 mm) are zoomed in and flatten distance.
Depth of field
Only one distance is sharp; nearer and further things blur. F-Stop sets how much.
F-Curve modifier
An effect on an animation curve, like Noise, which adds a random wobble.
Marker
A flag on the Timeline. Bind a camera to it and the film cuts to that camera there.
Cut
The instant a film switches from one camera to another.
Motion blur
The smear a real camera records when something moves fast during one frame.
Playblast
A quick, rough render straight from the viewport, for checking timing.
Establishing shot
The opening wide shot that shows where the story happens.

Part 1

Set up the film

Tools: File › Append…, the Output tab, the Outliner, the sidebar.

A capstone is built from what you already made. The car comes from Lab 11 and the volcano from Lab 17, copied in with Append. First, the shape of the film: 12 seconds, cinema-wide.

  1. A capstone folder and a fresh file

    Your computer’s file explorer first, then Blender’s File menu (top left).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Outside Blender, make a folder called Capstone. Copy (do not move) Lab11_Car_YourName.blend and Lab17_Volcano_YourName.blend into it. Your originals stay safe; the copies are what you build from.
    2. b
      In Blender: File › New › General. Click the cube, hover the viewport, X › Delete. Keep the Camera and the Light: they become your first camera and your sun.
    3. c
      File › Save As…. Go into the Capstone folder, name it Capstone_OpeningScene_YourName, click Save As.
    4. d
      From now on press Ctrl+S every few steps. Physics and fire are heavy: if Blender closes on you, a save costs you one step, not a lesson.

    You should now see: an empty grid with a camera and a light, and your file name in the window title.

    Why copy the old labs into the new folder?

    Append (Step 3) copies the car and volcano out of those files. Keeping all three .blend files in one folder means nothing goes missing if you move the folder, and if the capstone goes wrong you still have the originals to append again.

  2. Make it a 12-second widescreen film

    Properties editor (bottom right) › the Output tab, the printer icon.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Output tab › Format: Resolution X stays 1920. Resolution Y: 816.
    2. b
      %: 50 while you work. (Set it to 100 for the final render if your computer is quick.)
    3. c
      Frame Rate should already say 24 fps. Frame Range › Frame Start 1, End 288.

    You should now see: the Output tab reading 1920 × 816, 50%, 24 fps, frames 1 to 288. The Timeline along the bottom now ends at 288.

    Why 816 and why 288?

    1920 ÷ 816 = 2.35. That is the “scope” shape of cinema films: wide and short, which instantly makes a shot feel like a movie rather than a phone video. 288 frames at 24 frames a second is exactly 12 seconds: long enough for four shots, short enough to render on a school computer.

  3. Append your car

    File › Append…, then Blender’s file browser.

    Do this:

    1. a
      File › Append…. Find your Capstone folder.
    2. b
      Double-click Lab11_Car_YourName.blend. You are now looking inside it, at folders like Camera, Collection, Material, Object.
    3. c
      Double-click Collection, then click the item called Collection once. That is everything that was in your Lab 11 scene: Body, door, wheels, lights and all.
    4. d
      Click Append.

    You should now see: your Lab 11 car at the centre of the grid, sitting on its old 60 m ground, with a new collection in the Outliner called Collection.001.

    Why the collection, not just the Body?

    Appending the Body object alone leaves its parts behind: the wheels, door and lights are separate objects that only follow the Body because they are its children. A collection carries every part, every parent link and every material in one go. It also brings the arch cutter that your Boolean needs, so the wheel arches stay cut.

  4. Tidy the car’s collection

    The Outliner (top right).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Double-click Collection.001, type Car, Enter.
    2. b
      Open the arrow next to Car, then the arrow next to Body. Your Lab 11 camera is there as Camera.001 (it was a child of the Body).
    3. c
      Click Camera.001, Ctrl+click Ground, Ctrl+click Light.001.
    4. d
      Right-click one of them › Delete.

    You should now see: the Car collection holding only the Body and its parts. The grey 60 m square has gone.

    Why delete them?

    You already have a camera and a light, and you are about to build a 1 km world with its own ground. A second ground plane would fight with the new one (they would flicker where they overlap) and a second sun would double the light.

  5. Wipe the car’s old animation

    The 3D viewport and its Object menu; the Timeline (bottom).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Hover the viewport and press Shift+← to jump to frame 1. Every part parks where it starts: door shut, car at the centre.
    2. b
      Click the Body. Shift+click the Door, then Shift+click Wheel.FL (front left). Those three carried the Lab 11 keys.
    3. c
      Object › Animation › Clear Keyframes…, then confirm in the small pop-up.

    You should now see: no diamonds left in the Timeline. Press Space: nothing moves. (Space again to stop.)

    Why frame 1 first?

    Clearing keyframes removes the animation but leaves each part wherever it was on the current frame. On frame 60 the door would be stuck half open and the car 3 m down the road. Frame 1 is the “parked” pose.

  6. Append and place the volcano

    File › Append…, then the sidebar (N) › Item tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      File › Append… › Lab17_Volcano_YourName.blend › Object › click Grid › Append. Only the mountain: not its ground plane, target or camera.
    2. b
      F2, type Volcano, Enter. It has landed on top of your car: Lab 17 built it at the centre, 20 m wide.
    3. c
      Press N for the sidebar. Item tab › Location X 320, Y 30, Z -1.
    4. d
      Scale X, Y and Z: 18. It disappears. That is normal: Step 7 fixes it.

    You should now see: only your car. The volcano is now 360 m wide and 80 m tall, 330 m down the road, too far for the viewport to draw.

    Why so big and so far?

    In a movie the volcano is the backdrop, so it has to look huge from where the action happens. Lab 17’s volcano was a 20 m model you orbited around. Scaled 18 times it towers over the scene, and at 330 m away it sits behind the road in the opening shot and behind the burning car at the end. Z −1 sinks its flat edges just under the grass.

  7. Let the viewport see that far

    Sidebar (N) › the View tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      In the sidebar, click the View tab (tabs run down its right edge).
    2. b
      Clip End: 2000 m. The volcano appears in the distance.
    3. c
      Scroll the mouse wheel to zoom out until you can see the car and the volcano together.

    You should now see: your car at the centre and the volcano far off, glowing.

    What is Clip End?

    The viewport only draws things between Clip Start and Clip End, 1000 m by default. Anything further is cut off. Cameras have the same setting, and it bites in Part 8: each camera gets Clip End 2000 m too, or the volcano vanishes from the film.

End of Part 1. Save.

Part 2

Road, sky and sun

Tools: Add › Mesh › Plane, the Add Plane panel, an Array modifier, the World and Light tabs.

A set needs a floor, a road for the car and light that sets the mood. Four steps, all things you have done before, just bigger.

  1. Ground

    Add › Mesh › Plane, the Add Plane panel (bottom left of the viewport), then the Material tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Plane.
    2. b
      Straight away, open the Add Plane panel in the bottom-left corner. Size: 1000 m. (The panel closes on your next click anywhere else, so do this first.)
    3. c
      F2: Ground. Material tab (red sphere) › New. Double-click the name: Grass.
    4. d
      Base Color › Hex 4E5A2E. Roughness 1.

    You should now see: a dark olive plain stretching a kilometre in every direction, the volcano standing on it.

    Why not Scale it up?

    Size in the Add panel builds the plane 1000 m wide in the first place, so its Scale stays 1. Scale works too, but Part 6 gives the Ground physics, and physics behaves best on objects with Scale 1.

  2. Road

    Add › Mesh › Plane, the sidebar Item tab, the Material tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Plane.
    2. b
      Sidebar Location X -40, Y 0, Z 0.02.
    3. c
      Scale X 200, Y 3.5. A plane is 2 m across, so the road is 400 m long (X −240 to 160) and 7 m wide.
    4. d
      F2: Road. Material › New: Asphalt, Base Color 2B2C2F, Roughness 0.85.

    You should now see: a long dark road running straight from behind you towards the volcano.

    Why Z 0.02?

    Two flat surfaces at exactly the same height fight over which one to draw, and you get a flickering patchwork (“z-fighting”). Two centimetres is invisible in the film but enough to settle the fight.

  3. A dashed centre line, with an Array

    The Object tab (orange square), then the Modifiers tab (blue wrench).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Plane. Object tab: Location X -238, Y 0, Z 0.03. Scale X 1.5, Y 0.08. F2: CentreLine.
    2. b
      Material › New: RoadPaint, EDE6D2, Roughness 0.6.
    3. c
      Modifiers tab › Add Modifier › Generate › Array.
    4. d
      Count 54. Relative Offset › Factor X 2.5.

    You should now see: a white dash every 7.5 m down the whole road.

    How does Array know the spacing?

    Relative Offset is measured in copies of the object itself. Factor X 2.5 puts each copy 2.5 dash-lengths after the last: a 3 m dash, then a 4.5 m gap. 54 copies × 7.5 m = 405 m, the length of the road. Change one number and the whole line updates: that is the point of a modifier.

  4. A dusk sky and a low sun

    The World tab (red globe), then the Light in the Outliner and its green bulb tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      World tab › Surface: click the small dot to the left of Color and choose Sky Texture.
    2. b
      Sun Elevation 1°, Sun Rotation 16°, Aerosols 3. At the bottom of the panel, Strength 0.15.
    3. c
      Outliner › click Light. Green bulb tab: Sun, Strength 2.5, Color FFB27A. Sidebar Rotation X 80, Y 0, Z -16.
    4. d
      Hover the viewport, Z › Rendered.

    You should now see: an orange glow along the horizon, a dark blue sky above, long shadows, and the lava glowing on the volcano.

    Why dusk?

    Fire and lava only look bright against something darker. At noon the flames in Part 7 would look pale and the lava would vanish. A sun 10° above the horizon throws long shadows that show off every bump, and the warm sun against the blue sky is the orange-and-teal split from the Light, Colour & Texture lab.

End of Part 2. Save. A good place to end a lesson.

Part 3

Trees

Tools: Add › Mesh › Cylinder, Ico Sphere, Cone, Ctrl+J, M, Ctrl+A, the Geometry Nodes workspace.

Two hand-made trees, one hero tree for the crash, then a whole forest grown from the first two with Geometry Nodes. This is the one brand-new tool in the capstone, so it gets four small steps.

  1. A trunk

    Shift+C, Add › Mesh › Cylinder, the Add Cylinder panel.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Hover the viewport, Shift+C: the 3D cursor jumps to the centre (and the view zooms out; scroll back in).
    2. b
      Add › Mesh › Cylinder. In the panel: Vertices 8, Radius 0.22 m, Depth 3 m, Location Z 1.5 m.
    3. c
      Material › New: Bark, 4A3426, Roughness 0.9.

    You should now see: a thin brown 8-sided post standing on the grid. (It stands on top of the car for now; that is fine.)

    Why only 8 sides?

    You are about to make 250 trees. A low-poly look (few, flat faces) is a style many games and films use on purpose, and it keeps your computer fast. Lab 12’s pawn needed a smooth curve; a tree in the background does not.

  2. Leaves

    Add › Mesh › Ico Sphere, its panel, then Edit Mode and Mesh › Transform › Randomize.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Ico Sphere. Panel: Subdivisions 2, Radius 1.5 m, Location Z 3.8 m.
    2. b
      Sidebar Scale Z 1.25. Material › New: Leaves, 3F6A2C, Roughness 0.8.
    3. c
      Tab (Edit Mode), A (all). Mesh › Transform › Randomize: Amount 0.25 m. Tab back.

    You should now see: a lumpy green crown on top of the trunk.

    Why Randomize?

    A perfect sphere looks like a lollipop. Randomize nudges every point a different small amount, so the crown catches the light unevenly, like real clumps of leaves.

  3. Join it, pivot at the roots

    The viewport, then the Object menu.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click the leaves. Shift+click the trunk last (it becomes the active one, the brightest orange).
    2. b
      Ctrl+J: one object. F2: TreeRound.
    3. c
      Object › Set Origin › Origin to 3D Cursor.

    You should now see: one tree with its orange origin dot at its base, on the grid.

    Why the origin at the roots?

    The origin is the point an object scales and rotates around. The forest in Step 21 makes some trees bigger: around the roots they grow upward; around the middle they would sink into the ground. In Step 31 the crash tree leans over: around the roots it pivots like a real trunk.

  4. A pine

    Add › Mesh › Cylinder and Add › Mesh › Cone, their panels, the sidebar.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Trunk: Add › Mesh › Cylinder with Vertices 8, Radius 0.18 m, Depth 2 m, Location Z 1 m. Material: pick Bark from the dropdown next to New.
    2. b
      Cone: Add › Mesh › Cone with Vertices 8, Radius 1 1.6 m, Radius 2 0, Depth 2.4 m, Location Z 2.6 m. Material › New: Needles, 2E4F2A.
    3. c
      Shift+D, right-click. Sidebar Location Z 3.6, Scale 0.8 all three. Again: Shift+D, right-click, Z 4.6, Scale 0.6.
    4. d
      Click the three cones, Shift+click the trunk last, Ctrl+J. F2: TreePine. Object › Set Origin › Origin to 3D Cursor.

    You should now see: a round tree and a pine side by side. (If they overlap, that is fine: their positions do not matter after Step 16.)

    Why two kinds?

    Real woods are mixed. With two shapes, plus random sizes and turns in Step 21, no two trees in your forest look alike, and nobody notices they are all copies of two models.

  5. A Trees collection

    The viewport, then the Move to Collection menu (M).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click TreeRound, Shift+click TreePine.
    2. b
      Hover the viewport, M › + New Collection. Name: Trees. OK.

    You should now see: a new Trees collection in the Outliner holding just the two trees.

    Why their own collection?

    In Step 20 the forest is told “use whatever is in the Trees collection”. Add a third tree to that collection later and the whole forest starts using it too.

  6. The crash tree

    The viewport, M again, the sidebar.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click TreeRound. Shift+D, right-click: a copy in the same place.
    2. b
      M › Collection (the main one): the copy leaves the Trees collection. F2: CrashTree.
    3. c
      Sidebar Location X 0, Y 6, Z 0. Scale 1.3 all three.

    You should now see: a big tree on the left verge, 2.5 m off the road. Your car is still parked at the centre, next to it.

    Why Y 6?

    The road edge is at Y 3.5. The tree has to be off the road (or the car would be hitting it in the middle of the lane, which looks wrong) but close enough that a car swerving left reaches it in a fraction of a second. In Part 5 you will steer the car into exactly this spot.

  7. Forest ground

    Add › Mesh › Plane, the Object tab, then Ctrl+A (Apply).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Plane. Object tab: Location X 20, Y 40, Z 0.05.
    2. b
      Scale X 140, Y 30.
    3. c
      Hover the viewport, Ctrl+A › Scale. The Scale boxes go back to 1, but the plane stays the same size.
    4. d
      F2: Forest.

    You should now see: a big flat rectangle behind the crash tree, from 10 m to 70 m off the road.

    Why must you Apply the scale here?

    Step 19 scatters trees by area: “so many per square metre”. Geometry Nodes measure the plane itself, before Scale. Unapplied, your 280 × 60 m plane still counts as 2 × 2 m: 0.06 of a tree, so none at all. Worse, the trees would be stretched 140 times along X. We tested both. Apply first.

  8. Geometry Nodes: scatter points

    The Geometry Nodes workspace tab (top of the window), its node editor (bottom half).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Forest selected, click the Geometry Nodes tab at the top of the window. In the node editor, click + New. Two nodes appear, Group Input wired to Group Output.
    2. b
      Hover the node editor, Shift+A, type distribute, Enter. Drop the node on the wire between them (it slots in).
    3. c
      Density: 0.015.

    You should now see: the plane gone, replaced by a cloud of a couple of hundred dots.

    What are Geometry Nodes?

    A modifier you build out of boxes and wires, like the shader nodes from the volcano lab, except these make shapes instead of colours. Group Input is your plane; Group Output is what ends up in the scene. Each node in between changes the geometry on its way through. Density 0.015 means 0.015 points per square metre: one every 67 m², about 250 on this plane.

  9. Trees on the points

    The node editor.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Shift+A, instance on, Enter: Instance on Points. Drop it on the wire after Distribute Points on Faces.
    2. b
      Shift+A, collection info, Enter. Drop it below. Click its Collection box › Trees.
    3. c
      Tick Separate Children and Reset Children.
    4. d
      Drag from Collection Info’s Instances dot to the Instance dot on Instance on Points. Tick Pick Instance.

    You should now see: a tree on every point: round ones and pines mixed.

    What do the two ticks and Pick Instance do?

    Separate Children treats TreeRound and TreePine as two separate choices instead of one clump. Reset Children ignores where they stand in the scene (otherwise every tree would be shifted by however far the originals are from the centre). Pick Instance makes each point pick one of the choices at random. Without it, every point gets both trees, overlapping.

  10. Random size, random turn

    The node editor.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Shift+A, random value, Enter. Leave it on Float. Min 0.7, Max 1.5.
    2. b
      Wire its Value dot into Scale on Instance on Points.
    3. c
      Another Random Value. Switch Float to Vector. Max: X 0, Y 0, Z 6.28.
    4. d
      Wire its Value into Rotation.

    You should now see: a forest where no two neighbours match: some trees small, some big, all turned different ways.

    Why 6.28 and not 360?

    Geometry Nodes measure angles in radians. A full turn is 2π ≈ 6.28 radians (= 360°). X and Y stay 0 so the trees only turn around their trunks; anything else would tip them over.

  11. Hide the originals, add a second wood

    The Layout tab; the Outliner; the Object tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click the Layout tab at the top to get back to the usual screen.
    2. b
      Outliner: untick the checkbox next to Trees. The two originals (standing on your car) vanish; the forest stays.
    3. c
      Click the Forest. Shift+D, right-click. Location X 60, Y -90. Scale X 0.85, Y 0.25. Ctrl+A › Scale.

    You should now see: two woods: the big one on the left of the road, a strip on the far right where the town will go in front.

    Why untick, not delete?

    Delete the originals and the forest has nothing to copy: every tree disappears. Unticking leaves them out of the scene (and the render) but still in the file for Collection Info to use. The copy of Forest shares the same node tree, so it scatters the same kind of trees on its own patch.

End of Part 3. Save. A good place to end a lesson.

Part 4

A town

Tools: Add › Mesh › Cube, Object › Set Origin, Ctrl+R loop cuts, I inset, material slots, Alt+D.

“Some buildings nearby”: one building made properly, then copied five times. Lit windows make the town come alive at dusk.

  1. A block that stands on the ground

    Shift+C, Add › Mesh › Cube, Object › Set Origin, the Object tab, the Material tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Shift+C. Add › Mesh › Cube. Hover the viewport: G, Z, type 1, Enter. The cube now sits on the grid.
    2. b
      Object › Set Origin › Origin to 3D Cursor: its origin drops to the floor.
    3. c

    Step 25 insets each window by 0.9 m. With Scale still 5, 5, 9 the inset would be measured before scaling and come out 4.5 m wide on the sides and 8 m tall on the floors. Applied, 0.9 m means 0.9 m everywhere.

  2. Six floors

    A loop cut runs across the edge you hover. Hover a tall edge and the cut goes round horizontally, making floors. Hover a top edge and it would slice the building into vertical strips instead. The car lab used the same rule.

Drive and crash

Tools: the Timeline, I, T interpolation, the sidebar.

Keyframes, like the car lab, but now the car has somewhere to go. Eight keys tell the whole story: drive, swerve, hit, bounce, and the tree takes the blow.

  1. The car on the start line

    The Timeline (bottom), the viewport, the sidebar.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Timeline: click the frame box, type 1, Enter.
    2. b
      Click the car’s Body. Sidebar Location X -106, Y 1.75, Z 0. Rotation Z 0.
    3. c
      Hover the viewport, press I.

    You should now see: the car 106 m back down the road in the left lane, Location, Rotation and Scale boxes yellow, one diamond at frame 1.

    Why Y 1.75?

    The road is 7 m wide with its centre line at Y 0. Driving towards the volcano (+X), the left lane is Y 0 to 3.5, and its middle is 1.75. We drive on the left in Australia, which also puts the crash tree on the driver’s side of the road.

  2. Three more keyframes: the swerve, the hit, the bounce

    Timeline frame box, sidebar, I over the viewport.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Frame 132. Location X -18 (Y stays 1.75). Hover the viewport, I.
    2. b
      Frame 156: X -2.1, Y 4.8, Rotation Z 30. I.
    3. c
      Frame 164: X -2.6, Y 4.5, Rotation Z 34. I.

    You should now see: four diamonds. Scrub the Timeline: the car drives, swerves left off the road and ends nose-first against the tree.

    Where do these numbers come from?

    The car is 4.3 m long with its origin in the middle, so its nose is 2.15 m ahead of the origin. At frame 156 it is turned 30°: put the origin 2.4 m behind the trunk along that direction and the nose touches the bark (X −2.1, Y 4.8). At 0.67 m per frame the car is doing about 58 km/h. The last key rolls it back half a metre and twists it a few more degrees: that bounce is what makes it feel like a hit.

  3. Constant speed: Linear

    The Timeline, then the viewport.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Hover the Timeline and press A: all four diamonds turn orange.
    2. b
      Still over the Timeline, press T › Linear.
    3. c
      Hover the viewport, Space. Space again to stop.

    You should now see: the car at a steady speed the whole way, then a sudden stop at the tree.

    Why Linear? Try the widget below.

    Blender eases into and out of every key (Bézier), which suits a door or a camera. A car would crawl away from the start, slow down for no reason at frame 132, and drift gently into the tree. Linear keeps the speed constant between keys, so the crash is a dead stop: no braking, no warning.

Bézier or Linear?

The car’s Location X over the first 170 frames, with the same four keyframes. Switch, then play.

  1. Wheels that roll

    The viewport, the sidebar, the Timeline.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click Wheel.FL. Frame 1. Its Rotation Y should read 0 (X reads 90: that stands the wheel up, leave it). Hover the viewport, I.
    2. b
      Frame 156. Rotation Y 17000. I.
    3. c
      Hover the Timeline: A, T › Linear.

    You should now see: all four wheels spinning while the car drives, and stopping dead at the hit.

    Why 17000?

    Exactly the car lab’s maths. One turn of a 0.35 m wheel rolls 2 × π × 0.35 = 2.2 m. The car travels about 104 m before the hit: 104 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 47 turns, × 360° ≈ 17000°. The other three wheels still have their Copy Rotation constraints from Lab 11, so one pair of keys drives all four.

  2. The tree takes the hit

    The viewport, the sidebar, the Timeline.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click CrashTree. Frame 156. Hover the viewport, I (Rotation 0, 0, 0).
    2. b
      Frame 160: Rotation X -8, Y 5. I.
    3. c
      Frame 166: X -3, Y 2. I. Frame 172: X -5, Y 3. I.

    You should now see: the trunk whipping away from the car, springing back, and settling with a slight lean.

    Why do X and Y make it lean that way?

    The tree pivots at its roots (Step 14). Rotating about X tips the top sideways along Y, rotating about Y tips it along X. The car hits heading 30° left of the road, so it pushes the tree towards +X and +Y: X −8° and Y +5° together lean it the way it was hit. These keys stay Bézier: a springy, easing wobble is right for a tree.

Part 6

Smash the crates

Tools: two Array modifiers, Apply, P Separate, Object › Rigid Body, the Scene tab.

From the wrecking ball lab: a stack of crates on the verge for the car to smash through on its way to the tree. The car keeps its keyframes; physics does the crates.

  1. One crate, then six

    Add › Mesh › Cube, the Object tab, the Modifiers tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › Cube. Object tab: Location X -6, Y 4.3, Z 0.3. Scale 0.3 all three. Material › New: Wood, A0703C, Roughness 0.8.
    2. b
      Modifiers › Add Modifier › Generate › Array: Count 3, Relative Offset Factor X 1.05.
    3. c
      Another Add Modifier › Generate › Array: Count 2, Factor X 0, Z 1.05.

    You should now see: six wooden crates, three wide and two high, on the verge in the car’s path.

    Why 1.05 and not 1?

    Factor 1 puts each crate exactly touching the last. Physics treats touching boxes as already pushing into each other, and they jump apart on frame 1. A 5% gap (3 cm) lets them sit still until the car arrives.

  2. Six separate crates

    The modifier headers, then Edit Mode and the Separate menu (P).

    Do this:

    1. a
      First Array: click the small down-arrow in its header › Apply. Then the same on the second.
    2. b
      Hover the viewport. Tab, A, P › By Loose Parts, Tab.
    3. c
      All six are selected: Object › Set Origin › Origin to Geometry.

    You should now see: six objects in the Outliner, Crate to Crate.005, each with its origin in its own middle.

    Why so much fuss?

    A modifier makes copies that only exist for drawing; physics would treat all six as one rigid lump. Apply turns them into real mesh; Separate by Loose Parts turns each unconnected piece into its own object; Origin to Geometry gives each one its own centre to spin around. The wrecking ball lab’s wall needed the same three things.

  3. Physics jobs

    Object › Rigid Body, then the Physics tab for the Body, Ground and CrashTree.

    Do this:

    1. a
      With the six crates selected: Object › Rigid Body › Add Active.
    2. b
      Click the car’s Body. Physics tab › Rigid Body. Type Passive. Settings › tick Animated. Collisions › Shape Convex Hull.
    3. c
      Click the Ground: Rigid Body, Passive, Shape Box. Surface Response › Friction 1.
    4. d
      Click CrashTree: Rigid Body, Passive, tick Animated, Shape Convex Hull.

    You should now see: nothing different yet. Physics only happens when you play or bake.

    What does Animated do?

    A Passive body never moves by itself; things just bounce off it. Animated means “follow my keyframes, and shove whatever is in the way”. The wrecking ball lab’s word bank mentioned it; here it is the whole trick: your keyframed car becomes a battering ram. Friction 1 on the ground stops the crates sliding halfway to the volcano.

  4. Bake

    Properties › the Scene tab (cone and ball) › Rigid Body World › Cache.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Scene tab › Rigid Body World › Cache: Simulation Start 1, End 288.
    2. b
      Click Bake. Wait for the bar to finish; the button becomes Delete Bake.
    3. c
      Scrub frames 140 to 175.

    You should now see: the car ploughing through the crates and the crates tumbling and bouncing off across the verge.

    Why bake?

    Physics is chaotic: tiny differences send crates different ways. A bake records one run so every playback, every camera and the final render show exactly the same crash. In testing, crates sometimes flew 60 m; that is fine, they leave the shot. To change anything later: Delete Bake, change, Bake again.

Fire

Tools: the Relations panel, Object › Quick Effects › Quick Smoke, the fluid Physics panels, keyframes on a physics setting.

Labs 17 and 18 made smoke and fire inside a box. The same here, attached to the car, starting the moment it hits.

  1. A fuel pad on the bonnet

    Add › Mesh › UV Sphere, then the Object tab’s Transform, Relations and Visibility panels.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Add › Mesh › UV Sphere.
    2. b
      Object tab › Relations › Parent: Body.
    3. c

    4. d

    You should now see: a flat orange-outlined pad lying on the bonnet, wherever the car is on the current frame.

    Why set the parent here instead of Ctrl+P?

    Ctrl+P keeps the object where it is in the world. The Relations box does not: the sphere jumps, and its Location now means “metres from the Body’s origin, along the car”. So X 1.45 is always the bonnet, whether the car is at the start line or wrapped round the tree. You will use the same trick for the chase camera.

  2. Quick Smoke, at frame 170

    The Timeline, then Object › Quick Effects and its panel (bottom left).

    Do this:

    1. a
      Timeline: frame 170. (The car has stopped.)
    2. b
      FireSource selected: Object › Quick Effects › Quick Smoke.
    3. c
      In the Quick Smoke panel: Smoke Style › Smoke & Fire.

    You should now see: an orange wire box around the bonnet: the Smoke Domain. The Physics tab of FireSource now shows Flow settings.

    Why frame 170?

    Quick Smoke builds its box around wherever FireSource is right now. At frame 1 that is 106 m back down the road, and the fire would happen in an empty box over there. The domain itself does not move, so build it where the car ends up.

  3. Shape the box

    The viewport and sidebar.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click the wire box (Smoke Domain).
    2. b
      Sidebar Location X -1.4, Y 5.3, Z 3.9.
    3. c
      Dimensions X 6, Y 6, Z 8.

    You should now see: a tall box around the front of the car and the tree, its floor just under the ground.

    Why so tall?

    Fire and smoke can only exist inside the domain. Quick Smoke’s box is about 3 m tall: the plume would hit the lid and spread out flat, a dead giveaway. 8 m gives it room to climb. Every extra metre costs bake time, so it is only as wide as the fire needs.

  4. Domain settings

    Smoke Domain › Physics tab: Settings, Gas, Cache.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Settings › Resolution Divisions 64.
    2. b
      Gas › tick Dissolve, Time 30.
    3. c
      Cache › Frame Start 158, End 288.
    4. d
      Cache › Type › All.

    You should now see: a Bake All button under the Cache settings.

    Why start at 158?

    The car hits at 156. Starting the fire two frames later makes it look like the impact sets it off, and simulating only 130 frames instead of 288 halves the bake. Type All is needed for the Bake All button (the default, Replay, only simulates while you watch). Dissolve stops old smoke piling up against the lid.

  5. The burst

    FireSource › Physics tab › Settings; the Timeline.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Click FireSource (easiest in the Outliner, under Body).
    2. b
      Frame 158. Fuel 4. Hover the Fuel box (no click) and press I: it turns yellow.
    3. c
      Frame 172. Fuel 1.2. Hover, I.

    You should now see: two diamonds in the Timeline. (Nothing burns yet: Step 41 bakes it.)

    Can you keyframe a physics setting?

    Almost any box in Blender can be keyed: hover it and press I. Yellow means “keyed on this frame”, green “animated”, orange “changed but not keyed”. Lots of fuel for a moment, then less, gives a whoosh followed by a steady burn, the way a petrol fire catches.

  6. Bake the fire

    Smoke Domain › Physics › Cache; then the viewport.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Ctrl+S. Click the Smoke Domain. Physics › Cache › Bake All.
    2. b
      Wait. On a school laptop, a few minutes. When it says Free All, it is done.
    3. c
      Hover the viewport, Z › Rendered. Frame 150, Space.

    You should now see: the car hitting the tree and flames bursting off the bonnet two frames later, a smoke plume rising above.

    Why save first?

    The bake is written to a cache folder next to your .blend file. In an unsaved file it goes to a temporary folder that disappears. It is also the slowest step in the whole project: you do not want to lose it.

Part 8

Cameras like the movies

Tools: Empties, Track To, the Relations panel, Ctrl+P, depth of field, the Graph Editor, timeline markers.

A film is not one camera. It is several, each with a job, cut together. You will build four, each moving a different way, then tell Blender when to cut from one to the next. Try the camera lab first: it shows why each choice works.

Camera lab

Your crash site at the end of the film. Change the lens and the height, or pick a move to see what each shot type does.

  1. Shot 1 camera: aimed at a target

    Outliner, the camera Data tab (green camera), Add › Empty, the Constraints tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Outliner › Camera. F2: CAM_Wide. Camera tab: Focal Length 28 mm, Clip End 2000 m.
    2. b
      Add › Empty › Plain Axes. Location -40, 6, 10. F2: Target_Wide.
    3. c
      Click CAM_Wide. Constraints tab › Add Object Constraint › Track To.
    4. d
      Target › Target_Wide.

    You should now see: a dashed line from the camera to the Empty: wherever the camera goes, it looks there.

    Why aim at an Empty and not the car?

    An establishing shot shows the place: road, forest, town, volcano. Aim it at the car and the whole world swings around to follow a red dot. A fixed target 40 m ahead and 10 m up keeps the road and the volcano framed while the car drives into the shot.

  2. The crane move

    Timeline, sidebar, I; then Numpad 0.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Frame 1. CAM_Wide Location X -150, Y -14, Z 2.5. Hover the viewport, I.
    2. b
      Frame 72. Location X -138, Y -18, Z 16. I.
    3. c
      Numpad 0 (look through the camera), then Space from frame 1.

    You should now see: the camera rising from just above the grass to treetop height while the car drives in from the left.

    Why does a rise feel like a film opening?

    Rising reveals: from low, the near road fills the frame; as the camera climbs, the town, the forest and the volcano open up behind. It tells the audience “here is the world this happens in” before anything happens.

  3. Shot 2: the chase camera

    Add › Camera, the Object tab (Relations, Transform), the camera tab, the Timeline.

    Do this:

    1. a

    You should now see: the car low in the frame, the road racing past, the volcano ahead. The camera creeps closer as the shot goes on.

    Why no Track To on this one?

    Like FireSource, the camera is a child of the Body set through Relations, so its Location and Rotation are measured along the car. It rides with the car and keeps looking where the car looks, so it needs no target. The car lab parented its camera the same way: “that is how every car commercial is shot”.

  4. Shot 3: low, wide and in focus

    Why low and wide?

    A camera near the ground makes everything above it loom: the car looks bigger and faster. A wide lens exaggerates how fast things come at you. Depth of field (a soft background) is what makes a picture feel like it came from a real camera. The key at 133 is not for movement: Step 46 needs keys to hang a shake on.

  5. Shake on impact

    The Timeline, switched to the Graph Editor; its Channel menu and sidebar.

      Click CAM_Impact. Drag the Timeline’s top edge up to make it taller. Its editor-type menu (top left corner) › Graph Editor.

    Why only location, and why Restrict?

    The camera’s rotation belongs to Track To, so noise on rotation does nothing. Shaking its position a few centimetres while it keeps aiming at the car reads exactly like a jolt.     Why end on the volcano?

    The last shot of an intro should leave the audience with the bigger threat. The crash is over; the volcano is still there. Ending low and close, with the fire in front and the mountain behind, makes a frame you could put on the game’s title screen.

  8. Cut between cameras

    The Outliner and the Timeline; Marker › Bind Camera to Markers (Ctrl+B).

    2. b

    You should now see: four markers with camera icons along the bottom of the Timeline, and the view cutting from shot to shot as it plays.

    What is a cut?

    Each marker says “from this frame on, film through this camera”. Blender switches the active camera at each one, so one render produces a film with four shots. Real films do exactly this: different cameras, cut together. A cut on an action (the car about to swerve at 133) hides the jump.

  9. The movie look

    Properties › the Render tab.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Render Engine: EEVEE.
    2. b
      Tick Motion Blur.
    3. c

    You should now see: in the Rendered viewport, slightly richer colours and deeper shadows. (Motion blur only shows in the final render.)

    Why EEVEE and not Cycles?

    Cycles traces real light and would take hours for 288 frames on a school laptop. EEVEE renders a frame in seconds and handles the fire, the glow and the depth of field. Motion blur smears fast things the way a real camera shutter does; without it, a crash looks like a stop-motion toy.

Part 9

Render the movie

Tools: the Output tab, View › Render Playblast, Render › Render Animation.

Three steps from a .blend to a video file.

  1. Video settings

    Properties › the Output tab › Output panel and Encoding.

    Do this:

    1. a
      In the path box replace /tmp/ with //render/.
    2. b
      Media Type › Video. (Blender 4.2–4.5: File Format › FFmpeg Video instead.)
    3. c
      Encoding › Container › MPEG-4. Video Codec should already be H.264.

    You should now see: Media Type Video, Container MPEG-4, H.264.

    Why MPEG-4?

    Blender’s default container, Matroska (.mkv), often will not play on school computers or upload to SEQTA. MPEG-4 with H.264 is the ordinary .mp4 that plays everywhere. The two slashes in //render/ mean “a folder called render next to my .blend”.

  2. Preview the whole film

    The viewport header, its View menu.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Hover the viewport, Numpad 0. Click the Overlays button (two circles, top right) off so the grid and empties stay out.
    2. b
      View › Render Playblast. (Blender 4: Viewport Render Animation.)
    3. c
      Watch it through once like a viewer would. Turn Overlays back on afterwards.

    You should now see: a quick rough version of the whole film playing in a window.

    Why preview?

    A real render takes 15 to 40 minutes. A playblast takes one or two. If a cut is in the wrong place, the car is hidden behind a tree, or the volcano is cut off, you find out now and fix it in a minute instead of after a lesson of rendering.

  3. Render the movie

    The Render menu at the very top.

    Do this:

    1. a
      Ctrl+S. Render › Render Animation (Ctrl+F12).
    2. b
      Leave it alone until the counter reaches 288. On a school laptop: 15 to 40 minutes.
    3. c
      In your Capstone folder, open render: there is 0001-0288.mp4. Rename it Capstone_YourName.mp4 and play it.

    You should now see: your 12-second opening scene playing as an ordinary video file.

    What if it is too slow?

    Keep % at 50 (960 × 408). In the Render tab › Sampling, drop Render samples to 32. Render it over two lessons: set Frame Start to where it stopped and render the rest, then join the two clips in the Video Sequencer, or ask your teacher to leave it running.

End of the capstone. Save, upload, watch it with someone.

Something’s not right

Find the problem that matches yours

The car arrives in pieces, or with holes where the wheels go

You appended single objects instead of the collection. Ctrl+Z until it is gone, then Append › Collection › Collection (Step 3). If the arches are not cut, the ArchCutter came without its Boolean target: appending the whole collection fixes it.

I can’t see the volcano

Two possible causes. Clip End: sidebar › View › Clip End 2000 m (Step 7), and the same for every camera in Part 8. Or it really is not there: click Volcano in the Outliner and check Location 320, 30, −1 and Scale 18.

It’s all black, or the sky is grey

You are not in Rendered view: Z › Rendered. If it is black in Rendered view, World Strength is 0 or the Sky Texture is not connected: World tab, check Color says Sky Texture and Strength is 0.15. Sun Strength should be 2.5, not 2500.

The road or the dashes flicker

They are at the same height as the ground. Road Z 0.02, CentreLine Z 0.03.

No trees appear on the Forest

Usually the scale was never applied: click Forest, Ctrl+A › Scale (Step 18). Then check the nodes: Collection Info must say Trees, and its Instances must be wired to Instance on Points’ Instance socket (not Points).

Giant, stretched or lying-down trees

Stretched: Forest scale not applied. Giant: the Random Value for Scale has Max 15 instead of 1.5. Lying down: the vector Random Value has X or Y above 0; set both to 0.

Trees on the road or in the middle of the scene

On the road: the Forest plane is in the wrong place (Location 20, 40, Scale applied from 140 × 30). In the middle of the scene: the Trees collection is still ticked in the Outliner (Step 22).

One big window, or no windows

One big window per side: Inset was not on Individual (press I twice). No windows: the Windows material was never Assigned to the selected faces; Tab, select the faces again, highlight the Windows slot, Assign.

The car slows down before the tree, or crawls off the start line

The keys are still Bézier. Click the Body, hover the Timeline, A, T › Linear.

The wheels slide, or spin backwards

Slide: the wheel keys are Bézier (T › Linear on Wheel.FL’s keys) or you keyed the wrong wheel. Backwards: you typed −17000; make it 17000. Only Wheel.FL needs keys; if the others do not spin, their Copy Rotation constraints were lost: add one to each (Target Wheel.FL, only Y ticked).

The crates jump or fall apart on frame 1

They started touching or overlapping. Check the Array factors were 1.05, and that you set Origin to Geometry after separating. Delete Bake, fix, Bake again.

The car drives straight through the crates

The Body’s rigid body is not Animated, or not Passive, or the crates are not Active. Check all three, then Delete Bake and Bake again. A bake made before you changed anything still shows the old result.

No fire at all

In order: was Bake All pressed and finished (button says Free All)? Is the Cache Type All? Is FireSource’s Flow Type Fire + Smoke and Use Flow ticked? Are you on a frame after 158? Are you in Rendered or Material Preview view (Solid view shows only grey)?

The fire is in the wrong place

Quick Smoke was run on frame 1, so the box is 106 m back down the road. Move the Smoke Domain to Location −1.4, 5.3, 3.9 (Step 38), Free All, Bake All.

Smoke flattens against an invisible ceiling

It is hitting the top of the domain. Dimensions Z 8 (Step 38), Dissolve ticked with Time 30 (Step 39). Free All, Bake All.

The volcano is missing from a shot

That camera’s Clip End is still 100 m. Camera tab › Clip End 2000 m.

The camera never cuts

The markers have no camera bound: each marker should show a camera icon. Select the camera first, then Ctrl+B with the mouse over the Timeline, on the marker’s frame.

The whole shot shakes, or nothing shakes

Whole shot: Restrict Frame Range is not ticked on one of the curves. Nothing: the Noise went on Rotation (Track To overrides it) or CAM_Impact has no keyframes; key its Location at frame 133.

I got hundreds of pictures, or a file that will not play

Pictures: Media Type is still Image. A .mkv that will not play: Container is Matroska; set it to MPEG-4 (Step 51).

The render takes forever

Render Engine is Cycles: switch to EEVEE (Step 50). Keep Resolution % at 50. Close other programs. You can render in two halves (Frame Start/End) and join them.

I can’t orbit (no middle mouse button)

Drag on the coloured axis gizmo top right. Or Edit › Preferences › Input › Emulate 3 Button Mouse, then Alt+left-drag.

Check yourself

Eight quick questions

1Why did you Append the car’s whole collection, not just the Body?

Right. Children, cutters and materials all travel together in the collection.

The car is about twenty objects. The Body is their parent, but appending it alone leaves its children behind.

2Your volcano is in the right place but invisible. Most likely?

Yes. Anything beyond Clip End is not drawn, and the default is 1000 m for the viewport and 100 m for cameras.

It is Clip End: the viewport and every camera stop drawing beyond it.

3Why did the Forest plane need Ctrl+A › Scale before Geometry Nodes?

Exactly. Apply bakes the size into the mesh, so square metres are real.

Distribute Points on Faces measures the mesh before Scale. Unapplied, the plane is still 2 × 2 m.

4Why are the car’s keyframes set to Linear?

Right. A crash is a sudden stop; Bézier would slow the car down before the tree.

Bézier eases out of and into every key: the car would crawl at the start and slow down before the hit.

5What does ticking Animated on the car’s Passive rigid body do?

Yes. Your keyframed car becomes a battering ram for the crates.

Animated means “follow my keyframes, and shove things”. Without it the crates would ignore the moving car.

6Quick Smoke was run on frame 1 by mistake. What happens?

Correct. The domain is built around FireSource where it is at that moment and does not move.

The domain is built where FireSource is on the current frame, and it stays there.

7Why does Shot 3 use a 24 mm lens on a camera 45 cm above the road?

Right. Low angles make subjects powerful; wide lenses exaggerate speed towards the camera.

Low cameras make things loom, and wide lenses exaggerate how fast things come at you. That is the feeling of an impact.

8Your film plays through CAM_Wide the whole way. What did you forget?

Yes. Markers with cameras are what make the cuts.

Cuts come from markers with cameras bound to them. No markers, no cuts.
