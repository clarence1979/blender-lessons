Why real things look real

Look closely at any real mountain and you'll find detail at every size: the overall shape, the lumps and ridges, and the gritty surface of the rock itself. Computer models look fake when one of those sizes is missing.

In this lab you'll add each size of detail with a different Blender tool, then add light. Switch the layers on one at a time and watch the cross-section of your volcano change.

Cross-section of your volcano

Each button is one layer of realism and the Blender tool that makes it.

Word bank — open this if a word in the steps is new
Vertex
A single point on a 3D model. Edges join vertices; faces fill between edges.
Proportional editing
Moves the vertices near your selection too, so you pull smooth hills instead of spikes.
Falloff
The curve that decides how quickly that pull fades with distance.
Modifier
An effect stacked on an object that changes it without destroying the original mesh.
Texture
A pattern of numbers from black (0) to white (1) that other tools can read.
Node
One box in the Shader Editor. Wires carry numbers and colours from node to node.
Principled BSDF
Blender's all-in-one surface node: colour, shininess, bumps and glow.
Emission
Light that a surface gives out by itself, like a screen or hot lava.
Color Ramp
Turns a number from 0 to 1 into a colour, using colour stops you place.
Mask
A black-and-white map that says where one thing shows and where another does.
Fac or Factor
A number from 0 to 1 that controls how much of something you get. Older Blender calls it Fac, newer Blender calls it Factor.
Keyframe
A saved value at one frame. Blender fills in the frames between keyframes.

Part 1

Shape the mountain

Tools: Edit Mode, Circle Select and Proportional Editing. Tick each step as you finish it.

  1. Start fresh and save

    File › New › General. Click the cube, press X and choose Delete. Then File › Save As and name it Lab17_Volcano_YourName.blend.

    From now on, press Ctrl+S every few steps.

  2. Add a dense grid

    Add › Mesh › Grid. Straight away, open the Add Grid panel in the bottom-left corner of the viewport and set:

    X Subdivisions 200, Y Subdivisions 200, Size 20 m

    Why?

    A 200 × 200 grid has 40,401 vertices. Each one is a point you can push up or down, so more points means a smoother mountain and finer bumps later. It's the same reason you subdivided the cloth in Lab 15.

  3. Look down from above in Edit Mode

    Press Tab for Edit Mode. Click the blue Z circle on the navigation gizmo (top-right of the viewport), or press Numpad 7. Press Home so the grid fills the view, then Alt+A to deselect everything.

  4. Select the summit

    Press C for Circle Select. Scroll the mouse wheel until the circle is about one-seventh of the grid's width (left picture below). Click once in the exact middle of the grid, then right-click to finish.

    Why a circle and not one vertex?

    Lifting a whole disc gives the mountain a small flat top. You need that flat top in Step 9 to dig a crater into — a single vertex would make a sharp point with nowhere to dig.

  5. Turn on Proportional Editing

    Press O (the letter). The target-shaped icon at the top of the viewport turns blue. Click the small arrow beside it and choose Sharp.

    Why Sharp?

    Proportional editing also drags the vertices around your selection, less and less the further away they are. The falloff curve decides the shape of that fade, so it decides what kind of volcano you get. Try each falloff below.

Falloff explorer

These are Blender's real falloff curves. The settings match the Move panel you'll use in Step 7.

  1. Pull the mountain up

    Press G, then Z, type 4.5 and press Enter. Don't judge it yet. The spread is probably wrong, and you'll fix it next.

  2. Set the spread

    Open the Move panel in the bottom-left corner. Check that Proportional Falloff says Sharp, then set Proportional Size to 8 m. Hold the middle mouse button and drag to orbit around your mountain.

    The Move panel disappears as soon as you do anything else. If it's gone, press Ctrl+Z and repeat Step 6.

  3. Select the crater area

    Look from above again (Z on the gizmo) and press Alt+A. Scroll to zoom in on the summit first, then press C. Make the circle about two-thirds the width of the flat top (right picture above), click the middle once and right-click.

  4. Predict, then dig

    Change the falloff (arrow beside the proportional icon) to Smooth.

    Predict first: Proportional Size is still 8 m. When you push the crater down, what will happen to the rest of the mountain? Say it to the person next to you.

    Now press G, Z, type -1.5, Enter. Then, in the Move panel, set Proportional Size to 0.8 m.

    What just happened?

    With an 8 m circle of influence, the whole mountain sank along with the crater. Shrinking the size to 0.8 m keeps the dig inside the flat top, and the untouched edge becomes the crater's rim.

  5. Smooth it and save

    Press Tab to go back to Object Mode. Right-click the volcano and choose Shade Smooth. Save with Ctrl+S.

Part 2

Make it rugged

Tools: Displace modifier and a Clouds texture. This is the medium-sized detail.

  1. Add a Displace modifier

    In the Properties panel, open the Modifiers tab (blue wrench), then Add Modifier › Deform › Displace. Nothing changes yet, because it needs a texture to read.

  2. Give it a texture

    In the Displace modifier, click New. Open the Texture tab (the red-and-white chequered icon at the bottom of the tab column) and change Type to Clouds.

    Why?

    A texture is a black-and-white pattern. Displace pushes the surface out where the texture is white and in where it's black, like pressing a bumpy stamp into clay.

  3. Make the clouds rocky

    In the Clouds settings set Type Hard, Size 1.5 and Depth 6.

    What do these do?

    Size is how wide each bump is. Depth is how many layers of smaller bumps are stacked on top. Hard makes sharp creases like eroded rock instead of soft blobs.

  4. Set how tall the bumps are

    Go back to the Modifiers tab and set the Displace Strength to 0.8. Save.

    Why 0.8?

    Strength is how tall the bumps are. Above about 1.5 the volcano starts to look like a pile of crumbs, and the crater disappears.

End of lesson 1. Save and stop here.

Part 3

Rock and lava materials

Tools: Shader Editor, Principled BSDF, Noise Texture, Bump and Color Ramp. This is where the small detail and the glow come from.

This is the node setup you'll have at the end of Part 4. Come back to it whenever you're unsure what plugs into what. The coloured dots match Blender's: yellow carries colour, grey a number, purple a direction and green a shader.

Adding nodes: hover over the Shader Editor, press Shift+A and just start typing the node's name, then press Enter. To connect two nodes, drag from a dot on one to a dot on the other.

  1. Open the Shading workspace

    Click the Shading tab along the top of Blender. With the volcano selected, click New in the Shader Editor (the bottom half). A Principled BSDF appears, already connected to a Material Output. Double-click the material's name and call it Volcano.

  2. Make the rock dark and dull

    On the Principled BSDF, click Base Color and type Hex 4B4642. Set Roughness to 0.9.

    Why?

    Volcanic rock (basalt) is dark grey-brown and doesn't shine. High roughness spreads light out so there are no glossy highlights.

  3. Add tiny rock grain

    Add a Noise Texture and a Bump node. Connect Noise Factor (called Fac in some versions) to Bump Height, then Bump Normal to the Principled BSDF's Normal. Set:

    Noise Scale 120, Detail 10, Bump Strength 0.5

    Why?

    Bump fakes tiny dents using light and shadow without moving any vertices. It's the third and smallest size of detail, and it's cheap for the computer.

  4. Add a second Principled BSDF

    Add another Principled BSDF and drag it below the first. This one becomes the lava. Leave the first one alone — it's your rock.

  5. Add the lava pattern

    Add another Noise Texture. Change its 3D dropdown to 4D, then set Scale 30 and Detail 8.

    Why 4D?

    4D adds a value called W. Changing W makes the pattern slowly churn, as if the lava is moving. You'll animate it in Part 5.

  6. Colour it with a Color Ramp

    Add a Color Ramp and connect the new Noise Factor to the ramp's Factor. Then set up three colour stops:

    Click the left stop: Pos 0.45, keep it black.
    Click the right stop: Pos 0.65, colour Hex FFB52E.
    Click + to add a stop: Pos 0.55, colour Hex FF4D00.

    Why?

    The noise is just grey numbers from 0 to 1. The ramp turns low numbers into black crust and high numbers into yellow-hot lava. Drag the stops below to see how each one changes the look.

Lava playground

A copy of your lava nodes: Noise Texture into Color Ramp into Emission. Drag the stops, then switch on W to see what Part 5 does.

Selected stop: middlePos 0.55Hex FF4D00
W = 0.00

  1. Make it glow

    On the lava Principled BSDF, click the word Emission to open that section. Connect the Color Ramp's Color to the lava BSDF's Base Color, and connect it again to Emission Color. Set Emission Strength to 4.

    Why?

    Emission means the surface gives out its own light. Real lava is around 1,000 °C — hot enough to glow. Black parts of the ramp give out no light, so the crust stays dark.

  2. Test the lava

    Drag from the lava BSDF's BSDF output to the Material Output's Surface. Your whole volcano turns to lava! That's expected: Part 4 puts the lava only where you want it.

End of lesson 2. Save and stop here.

Part 4

Paint the lava flows

Tools: Vertex Paint, Color Attribute and Mix Shader.

You have two materials now: rock and lava. A mask decides which one shows where. It's a black-and-white map painted onto the volcano. Black means rock, white means lava. Try painting one here before you do it in Blender.

Mask painter

The volcano from directly above. Paint with your mouse or finger. Start in the crater, then run flows downhill.

Switch to The mask to see what Blender actually stores: just black and white. The Mix Shader reads black as 0 (rock) and white as 1 (lava).

  1. Switch to Vertex Paint

    Click the Layout tab at the top. With the volcano selected, open the mode dropdown in the top-left (it says Object Mode) and choose Vertex Paint. The volcano turns white.

    Why white?

    Blender has just created a colour layer that stores a colour on every vertex. It starts white, and it's saved inside your .blend file.

  2. Turn it all black

    Paint › Invert. The whole volcano goes black, which means all rock.

  3. Set up the brush

    In the bar along the top, check the colour swatch is white (click it and drag the brightness up). Set Strength to 1. Press F, move the mouse to resize the brush and click to confirm. Start with a brush about as wide as your finger on the screen.

  4. Paint the lava

    Fill the crater floor with white. Then paint 3 to 5 flows starting at the rim. Let each one run downhill, following the valleys and bending around bumps. Make them thinner near the top and wider near the bottom. Ctrl+Z undoes a stroke.

    Why follow the valleys?

    Lava is a liquid, so gravity pulls it into the lowest channels. Flows that cut straight across ridges look fake straight away.

  5. Hook the mask up

    Go to the Shading tab. Add a Color Attribute node and a Mix Shader. Then connect:

    Color Attribute Color → Mix Shader Factor (Fac)
    Rock BSDF → Mix Shader top Shader socket
    Lava BSDF → Mix Shader bottom Shader socket
    Mix Shader → Material Output Surface

    If the Color Attribute node's dropdown is empty, choose your colour layer from it.

    Why does this work?

    The Mix Shader uses the top shader when Fac is 0 (black) and the bottom shader when Fac is 1 (white). The soft grey edges of your brush strokes blend the two, like lava cooling into rock.

Part 5

Light it and animate

Tools: World, Sun light, Camera and keyframes.

  1. Make it night

    In the Properties panel open the World tab (red globe). Set Color to Hex 262B3D and Strength to 1. Press Z over the viewport and choose Rendered to see it.

  2. Set a low, warm sun

    Click the Light in the Outliner (top-right list). In the Light tab (green bulb) choose Sun, Strength 3, Color Hex FFEBD9. Then in the Object tab (orange square) set Rotation X 68°, Y 0°, Z 120°.

    Why low?

    A low sun throws long shadows, which shows off every bump you added in Part 2. A dark sky makes the lava look brighter by contrast.

  3. Frame your shot

    Orbit the viewport to a low, dramatic angle with the volcano filling most of the screen. Then View › Align View › Align Active Camera to View (Ctrl+Alt+Numpad 0).

  4. Animate the lava

    In the Shading tab, hover over the Shader Editor and press Shift+← to go to frame 1. Hover over W on the lava Noise Texture and press I. The field turns yellow, which means it's keyframed.

    Press Shift+→ to jump to frame 250. Set W to 3, hover over it and press I again. Press Space to play and Space again to stop.

    Why does it move?

    You saved W = 0 at frame 1 and W = 3 at frame 250. Blender fills in every frame between, so W creeps upward and the crust pattern churns. That's what the Animate W button did in the playground.

  5. Render and save

    Press F12. When the render finishes, choose Image › Save As in the render window and save a PNG named Lab17_Volcano_YourName.png. Save your .blend too.

    EEVEE renders in seconds. Cycles (Render tab › Render Engine) lets the lava light up the rock more realistically, but it's much slower.

Make it erupt with a smoke plume

This uses a domain, exactly like the water in Lab 16: smoke can only exist inside the domain box.

  1. Add › Mesh › UV Sphere. In its panel set Radius 0.5 m. Press G Z and lift it into the crater.
  2. With the sphere selected: Object › Quick Effects › Quick Smoke. A box appears. That's the domain.
  3. Select the box. Press S 2 Enter to double its size, then G Z 3 Enter to give the smoke headroom.
  4. In the Physics tab, set Resolution Divisions to 64.
  5. Select the sphere. In the Object tab, under Visibility › Show In, untick Renders.
  6. Select the box. In the Material tab, set the Principled Volume Color to a dark ash grey (Hex 3A3633).
  7. Press Space. The first play is slow because Blender is calculating every frame. Smoke shows in Material Preview or Rendered view.
Carve gullies with Sculpt Mode

Real volcanoes have deep channels running down from the top where rain and lava have worn the rock away.

  1. Switch the mode dropdown to Sculpt Mode and pick the Draw brush.
  2. Hold Ctrl and drag from the rim downhill to dig a gully. Do this 6 to 10 times, spread around the mountain.
  3. Hold Shift and drag to smooth any mistakes.
  4. Back in Vertex Paint, move your lava flows into the new gullies.
Volcano types: build a cinder cone and a shield volcano

Add two more grids next to your volcano (move them sideways with G X). Repeat Steps 3 to 7 with different falloffs:

  1. Cinder cone: Linear falloff, Move Z 3, Proportional Size 5 m.
  2. Shield volcano: Root falloff, Move Z 2, Proportional Size 9 m.

Render all three side by side and label which is which. Use the Falloff explorer in Part 1 to check your thinking.

Something's not right

Find the problem that matches yours.

Pressing G Z moves the whole grid, not a mountain

Proportional editing is off. Press O (the icon turns blue) and try again. Also check that only the small middle disc is selected: Alt+A, then redo Step 4.

My mountain is a thin spike or a giant block

A spike means Proportional Size is too small. A block with cliff sides means the falloff is Constant. Fix both in the Move panel (Step 7).

The Move panel disappeared

It only lasts until your next action. Press Ctrl+Z, then do the move again — the panel comes back.

I can't orbit (no middle mouse button)

Drag on the coloured axis gizmo in the top-right of the viewport. Or turn on Edit › Preferences › Input › Emulate 3 Button Mouse, then Alt + left-drag orbits.

Displace moved everything but there are no bumps

The texture type is still Image or Movie. Open the Texture tab and change Type to Clouds (Step 12).

It looks spiky, blocky or like crumbs

Strength is too high or Size is too small. Use Strength 0.8 and Size 1.5. Did you do Shade Smooth in Step 10?

The lava doesn't glow

Your viewport is in Solid view: press Z and choose Material Preview or Rendered. Then check the lava BSDF's Emission section: the Color Ramp must be plugged into Emission Color, and Emission Strength must be 4.

The whole volcano is still lava after Step 27

Either the lava BSDF is still plugged straight into the Material Output (it should go through the Mix Shader), or your mask is all white. In Vertex Paint, use Paint › Invert and repaint the flows.

I painted flows but no lava shows

Check three things: the Color Attribute's Color goes into the Mix Shader's Fac; the right colour layer is picked in the Color Attribute dropdown; and lava is in the bottom Shader socket, not the top.

My brush paints black

The brush colour is black. Click the colour swatch in the top bar and drag the brightness to white.

The lava doesn't move when I press Space

Check the lava Noise Texture is set to 4D, and that W has a keyframe at frame 1 (W = 0) and another at frame 250 (W = 3). Use ↑ and ↓ to jump between keyframes and check.

Blender is really slow

Stay in Solid view while modelling and only switch to Material Preview when you need to see colours. Close other programs. If it's still slow, start again with 120 subdivisions in Step 2 instead of 200.

Six quick questions

Pick an answer to lock it in. You'll find out straight away whether you're right.

1Why does the grid need 200 × 200 subdivisions?

Right. Every vertex is a point that can move, so more vertices means more detail.

Not quite. More vertices means the shape and the Displace bumps have enough points to show fine detail.

2Which falloff gives a stratovolcano's shape: steep at the top and gentle at the bottom?

Right. Sharp drops away quickly near the selection and then flattens out, giving a concave slope like Mount Fuji's.

Not quite. Sharp gives the concave slope. Linear makes straight sides (a cinder cone) and Sphere makes a dome.

3In the Displace modifier, what does Strength change?

Right. Strength is height. The texture's Size is width, and Depth is the number of layers.

Not quite. Strength is how tall the bumps are. Size sets how wide they are, and Depth sets how many layers.

4What makes the lava look like it's glowing?

Right. Emission makes the surface give out its own light.

Not quite. The glow comes from Emission: the ramp's colours go into Emission Color, and Emission Strength sets how bright.

5A white part of your mask sends Fac = 1 into the Mix Shader. Which shader shows there?

Right. Fac 1 uses the bottom shader, and Fac 0 uses the top one.

Not quite. Fac 1 (white) uses the bottom socket, which is the lava. Fac 0 (black) uses the top socket, which is the rock.

6Why did you switch the lava Noise Texture to 4D?

Right. Keyframing W from 0 to 3 makes the crust pattern churn over time.

Not quite. 4D adds the W value. Keyframing W from 0 to 3 is what makes the lava move.
